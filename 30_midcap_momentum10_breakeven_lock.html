Midcap Momentum 10 — No Stop-Loss, Just a Breakeven Profit-Lock

Same exact selection/weighting as reports 11-19, 24, 25, 27 — no downside stop at all. Instead: once a position has run up 30% from entry, arm a protective exit at cost — if it falls all the way back to breakeven before the next rebalance, exit immediately at 0%, rather than riding it down further or waiting.

● only 5.3% of positions ever triggered the rule ● near-neutral impact — a much smaller effect than either stop-loss threshold

This rule only ever fires for a position that BOTH ran up 30%+ from entry AND later gave essentially all of that gain back before the next rebalance — a much narrower trigger condition than report 27's fixed-percentage-loss stops. Over 2008-12-31–2026-08-24, that happened to just 19 of 360 positions (5.3%) — versus 15%'s 43.3% and 30%'s 12.5% of positions affected in report 27.

The result is correspondingly small: CAGR barely moves, from +40.6% (original) to +39.6% (with the lock) — a 1.0% point difference, far smaller than either of report 27's stop-loss variants — and max drawdown is essentially unchanged (-35.1% vs. -35.1%). This rule locks in exactly what it's designed to: guaranteeing a big winner can never fully round-trip back to a loss, without materially touching the strategy's overall risk/return profile.

Why this rule barely moves the needle

▲ framing

WHAT THIS SHOWS — the mechanism, not just the scoreboard.

Report 27's stop-losses fire on a SINGLE condition (price falls X% from entry) — easy to trigger, which is why 43.3% of positions got caught at a 15% stop. This rule requires TWO conditions in sequence: the position must first prove it can run up 30%, and THEN give essentially all of that entire gain back before the next rebalance. Most positions that reach +30% either keep running (never re-test entry) or pull back only partially — a full round-trip all the way to breakeven is a narrow, specific pattern, which is exactly why it only happened 19 times in 360 positions across 18 years.

What this rule DOES guarantee, on the 19 times it fired, is that a position which was once a big winner can never turn into an outright loss on the way back down — the average locked-out return was -0.3%, almost exactly breakeven by construction. Whether those specific 19 positions would have ended up positive or negative by the ACTUAL next rebalance (had the rule not intervened) isn't shown here — that counterfactual wasn't computed — but given how few positions are affected, either way the portfolio-level impact was always going to be small.

The practical takeaway: this is a genuinely different kind of rule than a stop-loss — it protects a specific outcome (never letting a confirmed winner become a loser) rather than capping downside broadly, and it costs almost nothing to add because it so rarely fires. It is not a substitute for a stop-loss if the goal is limiting how much any single position can lose — for that, report 27's 30% threshold remains the better-tested option.

Limitations

▲ read before trusting any number above

WHAT THIS SHOWS — every simplification behind this backtest.

  • Today's fixed NIFTY Midcap 150 constituent list is applied retroactively across the whole window (survivorship bias) — same disclosed approximation as every other reconstruction here.
  • Arming uses the day's intraday HIGH and the breakeven trigger uses the day's intraday LOW — a position that spikes to +30% and crashes back to entry within the SAME trading day is deliberately NOT caught (arming only takes effect starting the next day's check), a conservative choice since the data can't confirm which happened first within that one day.
  • The counterfactual — what the 19 locked-out positions would have actually returned by the real next rebalance had this rule not existed — was not computed; only the actual rule's effect on the portfolio's overall equity curve is shown.
  • Only ONE arming threshold (+30%) was tested — a lower threshold would arm more positions (and likely trigger more often); a higher one, fewer.
  • Zero transaction costs, slippage, or taxes are modeled — the extra 19 trades this rule generates beyond the original strategy's 331 would carry a small real-world cost not reflected here.
  • Equal weighting (not free-float market-cap x momentum score) and no F&O-eligibility screen, same as every other reconstruction in this project.
  • Prices are unadjusted; no dividends are modeled for any holding. This is a single, fixed 18-year historical path.
