Midcap150 Momentum 10 — Volatility-Scaled (Smooth) Exposure

Reports 42-64's regime filter flips 100%/0% the instant NIFTY 50 crosses its 200-day EMA. This report replaces the hard switch with a CONTINUOUS ramp: exposure scales linearly from 100% (at or above the EMA) down to 0% (a band_pct below it), removing the whipsaw problem at its root rather than tuning around it — there is no discrete state here at all, so there is nothing to "re-enter" or "confirm." Three band widths tested (10%/15%/20%) against the original and report 42's binary filter, all on the identical window.

● beats the binary filter on CAGR at every band width

Every band tested gives a HIGHER CAGR than the binary filter — +38.5% (binary) vs. +39.5% (10% band), +39.8% (15%), and +40.0% (20%) — while still cutting drawdown meaningfully versus no filter at all (-35.1%): -26.1%, -27.5%, and -28.3% respectively.

The trade-off: none of these bands protect drawdown as deeply as the binary filter's -23.5%. This forms a clean, non-dominated frontier — no filter (best CAGR, worst drawdown) → wider band → narrower band → binary filter (worst CAGR, best drawdown) — with every point on it a genuinely different, reasonable choice depending on how much an investor weights return versus downside protection, not a single "best" answer.

Why removing the whipsaw at its root pays off

▲ framing

WHAT THIS SHOWS — the mechanism, not just the scoreboard.

The binary filter's own honesty notes (reports 42, 46, 47) repeatedly found that its biggest hidden cost is 69 whipsaw round-trips — full sells and full buys triggered by ordinary volatility near the 200-EMA, all charged nothing in this frictionless model but expensive in reality. A smooth ramp has NO discrete flip to whipsaw on: a day where price wobbles slightly above and below the EMA just nudges exposure a few percentage points, rather than triggering a full round-trip. Removing that specific cost is exactly why the smooth version's CAGR comes out ahead of the binary filter's at every band width tested, even though the binary filter still protects drawdown more deeply (it goes all the way to 0% exposure at the depth of a real crash, which the smooth version with a narrow band width only approaches asymptotically).

This IS still a genuinely different mechanism worth weighing, not a strict improvement: the 3.3% of days at fully zero exposure under the tightest (10%) band shows it still fully de-risks during the depths of a real crash, just via a smoother path in and out rather than a single hard switch.

Limitations

▲ read before trusting any number above

WHAT THIS SHOWS — every simplification behind this backtest.

  • This is a SIMPLIFIED implementation: exposure is applied by scaling the ALREADY-COMPUTED fully-invested strategy's own daily returns, not by re-simulating actual partial share purchases day by day — in practice, continuously adjusting a real portfolio's stock/cash split would itself require frequent (small) trades, which this model charges nothing for.
  • Exposure uses YESTERDAY's close-to-EMA ratio to scale TODAY's return (disclosed in the function itself) — getting this ordering backwards was an actual bug caught and fixed while building this report; readers implementing something similar should watch for the same look-ahead trap.
  • Cash (the unexposed portion) earns exactly 0%, same convention as every cash-holding report here.
  • Only three band widths were tested (10%/15%/20%) — the true efficient frontier between "no filter" and "binary filter" could have better points elsewhere.
  • Today's fixed Midcap150 constituent list is applied retroactively (survivorship bias). Equal weighting, no F&O-eligibility screen, unadjusted prices, no dividends modeled — same as every other reconstruction here.
